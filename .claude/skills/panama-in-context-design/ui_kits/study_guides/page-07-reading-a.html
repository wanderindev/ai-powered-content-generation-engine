1.1 · The Founding

The first city on the Pacific

In August 1519, the Spanish governor Pedrarias Dávila founded Nuestra Señora de la Asunción de Panamá on the shore of the Pacific Ocean. It was the first permanent European settlement on the Pacific coast of the Americas, and it quickly became one of the most important cities in Spain's growing empire.

Its importance rested on three advantages:

  • A Pacific harbor from which expeditions sailed south toward Peru.
  • The silver route, carrying Peruvian treasure overland to the Caribbean.
  • A crossroads of peoples — Indigenous, Spanish, and enslaved Africans.

Within a few decades the city held stone churches, a cathedral, convents, and thousands of residents. Mule trains loaded with silver crossed the isthmus along the Camino Real, making Panamá a linchpin of transatlantic trade.

That same wealth, however, drew the attention of pirates and privateers who prowled both coasts — a threat that would eventually bring the city to its end.
