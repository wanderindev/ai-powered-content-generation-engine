Key Ideas

Why Panamá Viejo fell

  • Strategic wealth. The city was the Pacific terminus of Spain's silver route, making it a rich and tempting target.
  • The 1671 raid. The privateer Henry Morgan crossed the isthmus overland and overwhelmed the city's defenders.
  • Fire and flight. Much of the city burned; survivors chose not to rebuild on the same exposed site.
  • A fortified restart. In 1673 the capital was refounded two miles west on a defensible peninsula — today's Casco Antiguo.
  • A lasting ruin. The abandoned site became Panamá Viejo, now a UNESCO World Heritage archaeological park.

Casco Antiguo

UNESCO World Heritage

A city rebuilt behind walls

After 1673, Panama's capital rose again on a rocky peninsula ringed by defensive walls. Its narrow, cobblestoned streets blended Spanish colonial, French, and early republican architecture into the quarter we now call Casco Antiguo.

Today its plazas, churches, and the famous Golden Altar of San José make it an open-air classroom for Panama's colonial and independence history.

1.1 · The Founding

The first city on the Pacific

In August 1519, the Spanish governor Pedrarias Dávila founded Nuestra Señora de la Asunción de Panamá on the shore of the Pacific Ocean. It was the first permanent European settlement on the Pacific coast of the Americas, and it quickly became one of the most important cities in Spain's growing empire.

Its importance rested on three advantages:

  • A Pacific harbor from which expeditions sailed south toward Peru.
  • The silver route, carrying Peruvian treasure overland to the Caribbean.
  • A crossroads of peoples — Indigenous, Spanish, and enslaved Africans.

Within a few decades the city held stone churches, a cathedral, convents, and thousands of residents. Mule trains loaded with silver crossed the isthmus along the Camino Real, making Panamá a linchpin of transatlantic trade.

That same wealth, however, drew the attention of pirates and privateers who prowled both coasts — a threat that would eventually bring the city to its end.

1.3 · The Fall

Morgan's raid, 1671

By the 1670s, Panamá was famous across Europe for its riches. That reputation made it a prize for Henry Morgan, a Welsh privateer operating with the quiet approval of the English crown.

Rather than attack the fortified Caribbean ports directly, Morgan led some 1,400 men across the isthmus on foot. After a difficult jungle march, his force met the city's hastily assembled defenders on the plain outside the walls — and overwhelmed them.

In the chaos that followed, fire swept through the wooden buildings and much of the city was destroyed. Its residents chose not to rebuild on the same exposed site, and two years later founded a new, walled capital to the west.

Above: The bell tower of Panamá Viejo's cathedral still stands among the excavated ruins — the most recognizable survivor of the 1671 destruction.

Concepts

Reading the three cities

Historians often describe Panama City as three cities in one: the ruins of Panamá Viejo, the colonial quarter of Casco Antiguo, and the modern high-rise capital. Each layer marks a different chapter — and together they let us trace 500 years of change in a single afternoon's walk.

Key Terms

Camino Real
The "Royal Road" — the overland mule route that carried silver across the isthmus.
Privateer
A private sailor licensed by a government to attack enemy ships and ports.
UNESCO World Heritage
An international designation protecting sites of outstanding cultural value.

1.2 · The Silver Route

A crossroads of the empire

For nearly two centuries, the wealth of Spanish Peru did not sail directly to Europe. Instead it landed at Panamá on the Pacific, then crossed the narrow isthmus by mule to the Caribbean ports of Nombre de Dios and, later, Portobelo.

This overland leg — the Camino Real — was only about 50 miles, but it ran through dense rainforest, rivers, and mountains. Guarding it was a constant challenge.

At Portobelo, the treasure was loaded onto the great Spanish fleets. The annual trade fairs there were among the richest markets in the world.

Did you know?

A single mule train could carry silver worth more than an entire town's yearly earnings.

Control of this route made Panamá indispensable to Spain — and a permanent target for its rivals. The city's fortunes rose and fell with the treasure that passed through it.
